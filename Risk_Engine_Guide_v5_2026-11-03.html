Why this exists

An insurer collects premiums from customers and invests that money until it has to pay claims. The regulator, the National Bank of Belgium (NBB), wants to be sure that even if those investments lose value, the insurer can still pay everyone.

The European rulebook for this is Solvency II. In Belgium it is written into the law of 13 March 2016 on the supervision of insurance companies. It asks one central question: does the insurer have a big enough safety cushion?

In one line: the Risk Engine collects the insurer's investments, sorts them by type and risk, and prepares the figures that show how big the safety cushion must be.

The data journey

The work runs in two phases. Source data preparation gets the insurer's data ready in two stages, each taking the result of the previous one and adding to it. The calculation engine then uses that prepared data to calculate risk and reporting figures.

Pulls the list of investments from the source systems and sorts the shares.

Extract Data

The first stage gathers the raw material in three steps.

01

Collect the investment list

Copies the full list of investments the insurer holds. Everything after this works from that list.

02

Sort the shares

Picks out the shares and sorts them into three boxes by how risky and how easy to price they are. That decides how much safety money must be kept aside. See the three boxes.

04

Collect the accounting values

Takes the same kinds of investments and collects their values from the Belgian accounting books and from the international (IFRS 9) books, side by side in Excel, so differences can be explained. See why the values differ.

The three share boxes

Shares can crash, so the regulator asks the insurer to keep extra money aside for them. Riskier shares need a bigger cushion. Each box has a code from the Solvency II balance sheet report.

R0110Listed shares

Shares on a stock exchange. The price is visible every day and they are easy to sell.

Safety money kept aside: about 39% of value
R0120Unlisted shares

Shares in private companies. No daily price, and hard to sell quickly.

Safety money kept aside: about 49% of value
R0090Participations

Large stakes in related companies, often the insurer's own subsidiaries. "Part of the family" more than an investment.

Treated separately (strategic stakes about 22%)

Percentages are the standard Solvency II shocks before a small market-based adjustment. They show the size of the drop the insurer must be able to survive.

Instrument types

The engine works with five kinds of investment.

InstrumentIn plain wordsHow sure is the price?
BondsA loan to a government or company that pays interest and is repaid at the endUsually very sure
Convertible bondsA loan that can later be swapped for shares. Half loan, half shareFairly sure
FundsA ready-made basket of investments managed by someone elseDepends on what's inside

"Very sure" means the price is read straight off a stock exchange (accountants call this IFRS Level 1). "An estimate" means it comes from a model, which is the least certain (Level 3).

Enrichment step 1: loading the rulebooks

Before adding information to the investments, the engine loads the rulebooks that say how each investment should be labelled. They are kept in Excel files, so the business team can maintain them without changing any code.

Each run uses the rulebook version chosen in the menus at the start. A past run can therefore always be repeated with exactly the same rules.

Business rules

Decide which label each investment gets for each kind of report. Each rule also says which investments it applies to, for example only the shares and bonds on certain lines of the Solvency II balance sheet.

Translation tables

Turn short codes into readable descriptions: what kind of investment it is, how creditworthy the issuer is, and which country it comes from.

Enrichment step 3: splitting by segment

The insurer manages its investments in separate pots, called ALM segments. Each pot backs a specific group of customer promises, for example the life insurance business. To check that each pot has the right investments for its promises, every investment must sit in the right pot.

Some investments are recorded in one place but really belong to several pots. Mortgages are the main example. This step splits such positions across the segments using a distribution key, so each pot gets its fair share.

Mortgage portfolio€100m, recorded once
Distribution keye.g. 70% / 30%
Segment A gets€70m
Segment B gets€30m

Figures are illustrative. The real split comes from the distribution key used in the program.

In one line: step 3 makes sure each investment, or each share of one, is counted in the segment whose customer promises it supports.

Enrichment step 4: one rating per asset

A credit rating is a grade for how likely a borrower is to repay, from AAA (very safe) downwards. The same bond can have different grades from different agencies, or none at all. The risk calculations need exactly one.

This step uses a waterfall: an agreed order of preference. The engine looks for the first source in the list, and if that is missing it falls to the next one, like water running down steps. Every asset ends up with one rating, chosen the same way every time.

Preferred sourcenot available
Next sourceA
Rating usedA

The order of preference shown is illustrative. The real order is defined in the rating reference file loaded in step 1, which also translates the different agencies' grades into one common scale.

In one line: step 4 gives every asset a single, consistently chosen credit rating, which drives how much safety money bonds and loans need.

Enrichment step 6: fixed income type

Fixed income means investments that pay a regular, predictable income, mainly bonds. This step gives each of these positions a short label for the kind of borrower behind it, based on its Solvency II balance sheet line.

Balance sheet lineFixed income typeIn plain words
Government bondsSovereignMoney lent to states

The borrower type matters because the risk is different. A state is usually less likely to default than a company, and the regulator asks for less safety money on many government bonds.

In one line: step 6 labels each bond-type investment by who borrowed the money.

Enrichment step 7: liquid or not

An investment is liquid when it can be sold quickly at a known price. The insurer needs enough liquid investments to pay customers on time, even if many claims arrive at once.

This step labels each position using how sure its price is, the same price levels described earlier:

Price levelMeaningLabel
Level 1Price read straight off an active marketLiquid
Level 2Price derived from similar investmentsIlliquid
Level 3Price estimated with a modelIlliquid

The logic: if there is an active market quoting a price every day, the investment can be sold quickly. If not, selling it may take time or require a discount.

In one line: step 7 marks each investment as liquid or illiquid, so the insurer can see how much it could turn into cash quickly.

Enrichment step 8: applying the rulebook

In step 1 the engine loaded the business rules from Excel. This is where they are used. For each kind of label, the rules look at each investment and decide which value it gets.

Each rule says two things: which investments it applies to (for example only certain balance sheet lines) and what to do with them. Rules are applied group by group, for example the Solvency investment category, strategic or non-strategic participations, infrastructure type and the ALM risk card categories.

Kind of ruleWhat it does
If … thenWhen the condition is met, set the label.
If … then … elseSet one label when the condition is met, another when it is not.
DeleteRemove positions that should not be in the table.
Applies toshares and participations, but not bond funds
Doesset the matching Solvency investment category

A simplified reading of a real rule. The actual conditions are written in the rulebook itself.

Why keep rules in Excel?

  • The business team can change a rule without a developer.
  • Every change is visible in one file, not spread through code.
  • Each configuration keeps its own version, so past results can be repeated.

What changes in the data

Every position now carries the labels that reports need, all decided by the same rulebook. Later reports simply group and total by these labels.

In one line: step 8 applies the business team's own rulebook to give every investment its reporting labels.

Enrichment step 9: initial shocks

A shock is a "what if" change applied to the data, such as "what if share prices fell by 25%?". Applying shocks shows how the insurer's position would look after a bad market event, which is the heart of measuring risk.

Like the business rules, the shocks are kept in an Excel rulebook. Each shock rule says which positions it applies to and how to change them:

Kind of changeWhat it doesExample
ReplaceSet a new value, text or numberSet a rating to AA
AddAdd an amount to the current valueAdd 1.5 percentage points to a yield
MultiplyChange the value by a percentageLower a market value by 25%
Plain ruleApply a formula written in the ruleAny other adjustment
DeleteRemove positions from the shocked viewExclude out-of-scope positions
Market value today€1,000
Shock rulemultiply, −25%
Market value after shock€750

Examples are illustrative. The actual shocks and their sizes are defined in the shock rulebook.

In one line: step 9 applies the agreed "what if" scenarios to the data, so the impact of a market shock can be measured.

Assets only mode

Step 2: loading the investment limits

The insurer's investment policy sets limits: the most it may invest in one country, one government, one company group or one sector. This step loads those limits so that later steps can check the portfolio against them.

LimitThe question it answersExample
CountryHow much may we invest in one country, all issuers together?Germany: €1.9bn
SovereignHow much may we lend to one government?Italy: €670m
Issuer by ratingHow much may we lend to one company group? Better-rated groups get higher limitsAA: €150m, BBB: €50m (2019 values)
SectorWhat share of the bonds, of the shares and of the total may sit in one sector?Financials: 25% of bonds
Value at RiskHistory of the group's Value at Risk, month by monthMonthly figures since end 2018

Approved exceptions

Some holdings are allowed above the standard limits because a committee approved them. For example, bonds of Walloon, Brussels and Flemish public bodies approved by the ALCo in April 2018, or one loan fund approved for €170m. The tables record the approved amount and who approved it.

Limits change over time

Each limit carries a date. When the policy changes, a new row is added rather than the old one overwritten. For example, the AA issuer limit went from €150m (2019) to €110m (2022). The engine uses the limit valid on the reporting date.

Three extra risk measures

Beta

How strongly a share moves with the stock market. A beta of 1 moves with the market, 0.5 moves half as much, 1.2 moves 20% more.

WARF

The average credit quality of a fund, turned into one number. The higher the number, the riskier: an A-rated fund scores about 130, a B-rated fund about 2,700.

WARF for mortgages

The same measure for the mortgage portfolio, with its average remaining life: a score of 69 and about 14.5 years.

In one line: step 2 loads the investment policy's limits, the approved exceptions and three risk measures, so the portfolio can be checked against them.

Calculation engine

What the calculation engine answers

Everything up to now prepared the data. The calculation engine is where the real questions get answered. Remember the safety cushion from the start of this guide: the regulator wants the insurer to survive a bad year and still pay every customer. The engine works that out in three parts.

?

How big must the safety cushion be?

The engine imagines bad market events, such as share prices crashing or interest rates moving sharply, and measures how much the insurer would lose in each. Combined, this gives the SCR: the cushion the regulator requires. group 20

?

How big is the cushion we actually have?

The engine builds the balance sheet (what the insurer owns minus what it owes) and works out the capital that is really available to absorb losses: the AFR, available financial resources. group 30

?

Is the portfolio healthy day to day?

Beyond the regulatory figure, management wants a set of health checks: are the promises to customers covered, is there enough cash, how sensitive are we to interest rates? groups 40 and 41

→

Report the results

Finally the figures are put into the formats people need: the annual report and the limit monitoring report. groups 50 and 51

Putting the first two together: the solvency ratio

The headline number compares the cushion the insurer has with the cushion it needs.

Cushion we have (AFR)€1,500m
Cushion required (SCR)€1,000m
Solvency ratio1,500 ÷ 1,000 = 150%

Above 100% the insurer meets the requirement. Insurers usually aim well above it, so a bad year does not push them below the line. Below 100% the regulator steps in.

Figures are illustrative.

In one line: the engine measures the cushion needed (SCR), the cushion available (AFR), and the day-to-day health of the portfolio, then puts the results into reports.

Calculation engine · group 20

The files for the actuaries

The investment side is only half the story: the actuarial model (Prophet) projects the promises to customers. Group 20 therefore also writes the shocked asset values into scenario files (DCS and FAC tables), so the actuaries can run the same scenarios on the liability side.

In one line: group 20 asks "what if" for each market risk, measures the loss, and hands the same scenarios to the actuaries.

Calculation engine · group 20, step 02

Concentration risk: too much with one issuer

This step checks whether too much of the eligible portfolio is tied to one issuer or company group. A large exposure to one borrower can cause a big loss if that borrower gets into trouble.

1

Choose the eligible investments

Starts from the prepared asset list and keeps positions marked as eligible for concentration risk with a Solvency II value above zero.

2

Classify each exposure

Finds the issuer group and sorts each investment into a category such as covered bonds, property or government bonds. Currency, rating and other details decide the treatment.

3

Compare each issuer group with the portfolio

Totals the amounts per issuer group and category, then compares them with the total eligible portfolio and the threshold for that category.

4

Capital for the excess

For exposures above their threshold, applies the configured risk factors to estimate the capital needed. Non-rated insurance exposures follow special reference rules.

Detailed results

Exposure group, category, concentration level, threshold and the calculated excess and risk, so each result can be reviewed.

Overall result

The requirements per exposure are combined into one concentration risk figure for the entity and reporting date.

  • WARF factors. BBB appears with both 325 and 385, and NR uses 2720 (same as B). Worth confirming this is intended.

Classification rules

Each asset in the detailed list (S.06.02) rolls up into a balance sheet line (S.02.01) based on its CIC code and participation flag. CIC characters 1–2 show the listing country (XL = not listed), characters 3–4 the asset type.

InstrumentCIC (chars 3–4)S.02.01 rowTypical IFRS level
Government bond1xR01401
Corporate bond21, 23–29R01501 or 2
Convertible bond22R01502
Fund4xR01801 or 2 (3 for private equity)
Rights33R0110 / R01201 if listed
Shares31, 34R0110 / R0120 / R00901 listed, 3 unlisted
 
if participation:
    row = "R0090"          # holdings in related undertakings
elif cic[2] == "3":       # category 3 = equity
    row = "R0120" if cic[:2] in ("XL", "XT") else "R0110"

R0100 (Equities) is the subtotal of R0110 + R0120, so it is excluded from filters to avoid double counting. Values are in column C0010 (Solvency II value).

Validation checks

Rules that can be automated to catch misclassified assets:

Listing vs price level

  • R0110 assets flagged IFRS Level 3: investigate.
  • R0120 assets flagged Level 1: CIC listing code is probably wrong.

Reconciliation

  • Sum of S.06.02 per row equals the S.02.01 total.
  • Gaps between BGAAP, IFRS 9 and Solvency II values are explained per instrument.
